Chakra — Swarm Robotics Platform (In Progress)

A multi-robot swarm platform, starting with one robot: Rudra, the "queen," built on the chassis from my Apollo 1 rover. It drives itself around obstacles using a camera and a time-of-flight sensor together, pivots and drives in a straight line reliably after fixing two real motor-stall bugs, recovers on its own from a Bluetooth link going silently dead, and I can start, stop, and watch it from a touchscreen panel over WiFi.

github.com/dhruvauppaluri/swarm-project

Project Overview

  • Role: solo project, ongoing
  • Status: drive (forward, backward, and pivot-in-place), distance sensing, and camera-based obstacle avoidance are all verified reliable live on the first robot; Bluetooth staleness now has a real auto-recovery watchdog instead of a manual restart; a touchscreen control panel is flashed and verified live end-to-end; a MuJoCo sim-to-real RL pipeline trains a policy that avoids obstacles reliably in simulation (0.5% collision rate over 400 randomized test layouts, after finding and fixing a real bug and a real reward-design flaw, see below); the sim policy hasn't been deployed to the real robot yet, and swarm/multi-robot logic hasn't started
  • Key hardware: the chassis from the Apollo 1 rover build, rebuilt around 2-motor differential drive: ESP32-WROOM-32 DevKitC, a single L298N H-bridge, a fixed-mount VL53L0X time-of-flight sensor, a Seeed XIAO ESP32S3 Sense camera board, and a SeeedStudio SenseCAP Indicator D1 (ESP32-S3 + RP2040, 480×480 touchscreen) as a control panel
  • Stack: Arduino/C++ firmware, Python (FastAPI, WebSocket, OpenCV, YOLOv8n via ultralytics, pyserial), LVGL for the touchscreen UI, macOS blueutil for Bluetooth session management, MuJoCo + Gymnasium + Stable-Baselines3 (PPO) for the sim-to-real training pipeline
  • Repository: github.com/dhruvauppaluri/swarm-project

Note: this builds on Apollo 1, the WeaveHacks 4 EEG rover: I'm reusing that chassis, but everything on top of it is a fresh rebuild in its own swarm-project repo. The Arduino Uno and per-motor transistors are gone; it's an ESP32 platform now, with a new drive layout, a WebSocket/Bluetooth bridge, a camera-based perception stack, and a touchscreen panel. I'd originally named this robot "Apollo1" too, which got confusing fast (same name, unrelated robot, nothing but a recycled frame in common), so it's since been renamed Rudra, and the platform itself is now Chakra — a name that actually started life as the software arbitration layer that lets manual control and the camera's vision take turns driving (see below), before it made sense as the name for the whole thing.

Objective

The eventual goal is a colony of small robots coordinating as a swarm. That's a long way off, and I don't think it's worth chasing until one robot can reliably drive, sense, avoid obstacles, and stay reachable on its own. So that's what Rudra has been: the old chassis, rebuilt with a simpler drive layout, real perception, and enough control surfaces (a web D-pad, now a touchscreen panel) to actually trust it before I start on a second robot.

It's paid off. What started as a hardware bring-up exercise is now a robot that drives itself around obstacles with a camera, pivots and drives straight reliably, recovers on its own from a dead Bluetooth link, and I can start, stop, and watch it from a touchscreen over WiFi. Getting multiple robots talking to each other is still the next big step, and I haven't started on it yet.

System architecture

  • Chassis: the platform from the Apollo 1 rover build, rebuilt around a single L298N H-bridge driving 2 motors in differential drive, simpler and more reliable than the 4-motor/2-module skid-steer layout it replaced, after a persistent single-channel wiring fault on the old layout.
  • Drive: latched commands (w/a/s/d/x) over both USB serial and Bluetooth SPP, mirrored to both at once so either link can debug the other. Forward/backward and pivot-in-place both use a staggered "soft-start" — each motor ramps up (or, for a straight-line breakaway, briefly pulses to full power) slightly offset from the other — so the two motors' current draw never spikes at the exact same instant (see Debugging below for why that mattered).
  • Distance sensing: a fixed-forward VL53L0X time-of-flight sensor, plus a firmware-side autonomous obstacle-avoidance state machine that backs up and turns away from anything it gets too close to, regardless of what it is.
  • Camera-based obstacle avoidance: a Seeed XIAO ESP32S3 Sense streams MJPEG over its own WiFi link; a Python script runs YOLOv8n on the live feed, decides a drive command from detected-object position and size, and fuses it with the VL53L0X reading: the point sensor can only escalate caution, never override the camera toward "clear." A point-blank reading now hands off to the firmware's own backup-and-turn recovery instead of just stopping, since vision alone can't steer away from anything it wasn't trained to recognize.
  • Control bridge & arbitration: a FastAPI + WebSocket server (control/web_ui/server.py) owns the single Bluetooth serial connection to the robot and fans it out to any number of clients at once — the browser D-pad, the vision script, and the touchscreen panel all share the same link without fighting over the serial port. In front of it, chakra.py arbitrates between manual and vision control: a manual command always wins immediately, then hands control back to vision automatically after a short idle period, so nobody has to remember to release control by hand.
  • Bluetooth reliability: the link can go silently stale (accepts writes, nothing ever comes back, the robot itself stays alive) — a firmware heartbeat and a host-side watchdog now detect that and force a clean reconnect on their own, instead of needing someone to notice and restart the bridge server by hand.
  • Touchscreen control panel: a SeeedStudio SenseCAP Indicator D1 (ESP32-S3 + RP2040 co-processor, 480×480 touchscreen) runs an LVGL UI with a tabbed layout: a live start/stop + status tab for Rudra, and a reserved placeholder tab for the next robot. Since the Indicator's ESP32-S3 only has BLE radio hardware (no Classic Bluetooth), it reaches the robot over WiFi as a WebSocket client of the same bridge server the browser D-pad uses.
  • Sim-to-real RL: a MuJoCo model built to the robot's real measured dimensions, wrapped as a Gymnasium environment with the same discrete drive commands and sensor readings (VL53L0X + a rangefinder fan standing in for the camera's field of view) the real robot actually has, trained with PPO via Stable-Baselines3. The idea is to train obstacle-avoidance robustness in simulation before ever risking it on the real hardware: the policy now reliably avoids obstacles in simulation, but hasn't been deployed to the real robot yet.

Implementation

Chassis redesign: from 4-motor skid-steer to 2-motor differential drive

Rudra started on the same 4WD chassis as Apollo 1, driven by two L298N modules in skid-steer. One motor channel just wouldn't spin, and a swap test made it obvious this wasn't the motor or the driver: a known-good motor failed on that same channel, and the "bad" motor was fine everywhere else, so it had to be a wiring fault I didn't want to chase down further. I dropped the front two wheels for a free-swiveling caster and rebuilt the drive around a single L298N and two rear motors instead. Simpler electronics, one less thing to go wrong. And once the camera gave the robot wide-field awareness with no moving parts, the servo that used to pan the distance sensor didn't have a job anymore, so I retired it for good.

Fixing pivot-in-place and straight-line breakaway stalls

For a while, pivot commands would spin one wheel and leave the other completely stationary — not weak, fully stalled — even at a duty well above what straight-line driving needed. Both motors tested perfectly healthy on their own, so it wasn't a wiring or motor fault. The actual cause: a DC motor's inrush current when it first breaks static friction is far higher than its running current, and commanding both motors to jump straight to full duty in the same instant asked for more combined peak current than the shared L298N and battery rail could deliver — whichever motor's voltage sagged first would stall outright, and a stalled motor draws even more current, starving the other side further. The fix was to stagger each motor's onset by a few milliseconds and ramp each one up rather than snap to full duty, so the two current spikes never land at the same moment. Verified over 18 straight clean pivot trials, in both directions, elevated and on the floor, with and without the camera board's extra weight and power draw.

Chasing that bug surfaced a second, unrelated one: straight-line driving stalled unpredictably too — sometimes the left wheel needed a manual kick to start, sometimes the right, with no pattern and no loose wiring behind it. Turned out the baseline drive duty was simply too close to these motors' own breakaway-friction torque, so which one caught first was close to a coin flip. Raising the baseline duty (to the same value already proven reliable for pivot) and adding a brief, staggered full-power "kick" before settling into cruising speed fixed it — verified clean across repeated forward and backward trials, elevated and under load.

Bluetooth heartbeat and the Chakra arbitration layer

The Bluetooth link had a long-standing failure mode where it would go quiet — the OS reports it as connected, writes succeed, and nothing ever comes back, with the robot itself still alive. The bridge server had no way to tell the difference between "quiet because nothing's happening" and "quiet because the link is dead," so the only fix was noticing and restarting it by hand. I added a trivial heartbeat command to the firmware and a watchdog on the host side: if there's been silence for a while, ping once, and if that goes unanswered, force a full reconnect. I got to verify it does exactly what it's supposed to sooner than expected — a firmware reflash mid-session killed the link for real, and the watchdog caught it and reconnected on its own without me touching anything.

Around the same time I built chakra.py, a small orchestration layer that sits between the bridge and anything that wants to drive: the browser D-pad, the vision script, eventually a trained policy. A manual command always takes over immediately; if nothing manual comes in for a few seconds, control reverts to vision automatically. It's the piece that made "Chakra" feel like the right name for the whole platform, not just this one layer.

Camera-based obstacle avoidance, and its blind spot

control/vision/object_avoidance.py runs YOLOv8n on the XIAO ESP32S3 Sense's live MJPEG stream. It turns where an object sits in frame and how big it looks into a drive command, then cross-checks that against the VL53L0X reading over serial. Testing it live surfaced something I hadn't considered: I held my hand in front of the robot and it never steered around it at all, just stopped in place. Vision only reacts to a fixed list of trained object classes, and a bare hand isn't reliably one of them — so vision reported "clear" the entire time, and the distance sensor (a single fixed beam with no side-to-side awareness) was only ever wired to stop, never to turn. Nothing in the pipeline could decide which way to turn from something it couldn't classify, which matters a lot for a robot meant to live in an actual house full of things that will never all be on a trained object list. The fix: a point-blank distance reading now hands off to the firmware's own already-built backup-and-turn recovery instead of just stopping and waiting — class-agnostic, so it reacts to closeness alone regardless of what's actually there.

SenseCAP Indicator touchscreen panel

This one took a while to get right. The touchscreen sketch (firmware/sensecap_display_control) draws an LVGL tab view with START/STOP buttons and a status label that just mirrors whatever the robot's own firmware last printed, forwarded through the WebSocket bridge as plain text. Before I flashed anything, I checked what was actually already running on the board, and it turned out to still be someone else's old hackathon demo: an air-quality logger on the RP2040 side, and a WiFi app on the ESP32-S3 that I could see live over serial and, read-only, straight out of the flash image. Once I got to actually flashing my own sketch, the upload kept failing at the default baud rate (921600 corrupted mid-transfer on this board's USB path). Dropping to 115200 fixed it. Getting from "screen renders" to "screen actually shows live data" took one more real bug, in the bridge server itself (see below).

Display and touch bring-up (the ST7701 panel init, the PCA9555 IO-expander wiring, the software-SPI bus, the LVGL↔Arduino_GFX glue) isn't mine: it's adapted near-verbatim from LongDirtyAnimAlf/SenseCap, which is the reference implementation Seeed's own wiki points to for this board. The touch driver is mmMicky/TouchLib, also linked from Seeed's docs. What's mine is the WiFi/WebSocket client, the start/stop/status UI, and the tabbed layout for multiple robots.

Sim-to-real RL: MuJoCo + PPO

The plan is to train obstacle-avoidance robustness in simulation before ever putting a half-trained policy on real hardware. control/sim/ generates a MuJoCo model from the robot's actual measured dimensions (wheelbase, wheel size, chassis footprint, VL53L0X mount height), wraps it as a Gymnasium environment, and trains against it with PPO via Stable-Baselines3. The action space is the same five discrete commands the firmware accepts, not raw wheel velocities, and the observation is the same sensor readings the real robot has (a rangefinder standing in for the VL53L0X, plus a fan of others approximating the camera's field of view), specifically so a trained policy isn't leaning on information the real robot doesn't have.

This took three real bugs and one real research-shaped problem to get to a policy that actually works, and I'm glad I didn't stop at "the reward curve looks good" for any of them.

First, a reward-hacking bug: the earliest training run looked great on paper (reward climbing, episodes surviving to the step cap) until I actually watched the policy drive and realized it had learned to shuffle forward and backward in place near its spawn point, which happens to be far enough from any obstacle to never crash. The reward was paying for raw distance traveled, so wiggling scored the same as real progress. Fixed by only rewarding progress in the direction the robot's actually facing.

Then a much bigger one: every policy after that just drove dead straight until it hit something, no matter how I tuned the reward or the training hyperparameters. I eventually stepped below the RL framework entirely and logged the raw sensor values frame-by-frame during a rollout: every rangefinder reading was pinned at max range, every single step, right up until the moment of collision. The sensors were pointed backward. Two separate bugs in how I was orienting the MuJoCo sensor sites: a sign error, plus an angular-sweep calculation that (because of how MuJoCo composes its Euler rotations) had zero actual effect on ray direction regardless of the angle I asked for. Every policy up to that point had been training completely blind: it wasn't failing to learn to turn, it had nothing to turn away from. Switched from Euler angles to MuJoCo's zaxis attribute, which sets ray direction directly, and verified the fix by checking raw sensor output against known wall distances before trusting a single training run again.

With real sensing, a policy that finally worked, with a much harder problem hiding underneath it. Training now clearly worked (zero collisions across 150 random test layouts), but resuming an already-good checkpoint for more training sometimes made it more dangerous, not less: once, dramatically, from 1-in-150 collisions to 13-in-150. My first guess was training instability, so I added PPO's trust-region safeguard (target_kl) and tuned the batch/epoch counts. The checkpoint still got worse, with the stability metrics staying perfectly calm the entire time. That ruled out "noisy training" and pointed at something else: the reward function's own math. A fast, confident policy could bank enough reward per episode that occasionally eating a collision penalty was still, in expectation, a better trade than driving cautiously: the algorithm wasn't misbehaving, it was correctly solving the reward exactly as I'd written it. Raised the collision penalty and added a steeper penalty for genuine near-misses, plus a training curriculum that deliberately practices the tight-gap layouts most likely to catch a policy out. The result: 2 collisions out of 400 randomized test layouts, and zero out of a fixed set of deliberately hard ones I built to regression-test the exact failure modes I'd found.

It's not a fully closed problem: there's a real tradeoff between a faster, more confident checkpoint and a more cautious, more reliable one, and closing that gap for good probably needs reward-function work beyond what I've tried so far. There's also a real gap I haven't started closing: the sim's clean, wide-field rangefinder fan doesn't match the real robot's actual sensing (a narrow single-beam distance sensor plus a vision model that only recognizes a fixed list of objects) — a policy trained on idealized sensor data may not handle the real system's blind spots, the same ones the camera pipeline above just ran into with a bare hand. Worth a proper look once I've got real motion data (turn rate, speed) from an IMU to also replace the sim's remaining placeholder values.

Debugging & iteration

Pivot-in-place: one wheel fully stalled, not just weak

Covered above under Implementation, but worth calling out on its own: this is the same class of bug as two other power issues I'd already run into on this build (an over-discharged battery, and two power sources fighting on one rail) — a shared electrical resource getting oversubscribed by two simultaneous demands. What made it tricky to spot was that it looked exactly like a per-motor weakness (one wheel just doesn't spin) when it was actually a timing collision between both motors' current demand. The tell was that both motors tested perfectly healthy alone; it only ever failed when asked to move together, in opposite directions, at the same instant.

Straight-line stall: a coin-flip, not a fault

This one looked like it should have been the same bug wearing a different hat, and I almost treated it that way. It wasn't — I checked by testing each motor completely alone, no shared current demand at all, and it still failed to self-start randomly. That ruled out a current-sharing problem and pointed at something simpler: the base drive duty was just sitting right at the edge of these motors' own breakaway torque, where whether a given attempt succeeds is close to random. Two symptoms that looked identical ("a wheel didn't spin") turned out to need completely different fixes, and the only way to tell them apart was isolating each motor by itself before assuming the first bug's explanation covered the second one too.

SenseCAP display: the bridge kept dropping the connection right after handshake

Once the panel was flashed, I couldn't tell if it was working from serial: turns out Serial output just doesn't route over this board's USB path at runtime, so the only real signal was the screen itself, which was at least rendering. But it connected to the bridge and dropped again immediately, over and over, three times in under ten seconds. The server-side traceback gave it away: the bridge sent its first status message to a client before it had even entered its own try/except block, so when the ESP32's WebSocket library closed the connection right after the handshake (apparently it needs a beat before it's ready to receive anything), the exception went uncaught and the dead client just sat there. I moved that first send inside the try and added a short delay before it, and that was it: the connection held for 25+ seconds straight afterward instead of dying every few. I knew it actually worked once the display's status label started showing real live data from the bridge instead of a placeholder string.

Bluetooth SPP: three stacked bugs, then a fourth I finally closed

Getting Bluetooth SPP to reconnect cleanly took three separate fixes, and I only found any of them by reading raw system logs instead of guessing from what I was seeing on screen: macOS caching a stale RFCOMM channel after a reboot (fixed by forcing a fresh unpair → pair → connect before every session), the ESP32's own bond store disagreeing with a freshly re-paired host key (fixed by clearing the firmware's bond on every disconnect, not just at boot), and a macOS race where pairing would finish before the device's name resolved, which silently killed the serial port even though the Bluetooth link itself was fine (fixed with a short discovery scan in between). A fourth failure mode sat unsolved for a while: sometimes the link just goes quiet — reports connected, accepts writes, and nothing ever comes back. It's not any of the earlier three; those all fail loudly somewhere specific. I finally closed it with a heartbeat: ping after a stretch of silence, force a reconnect if nothing answers. Confirmed it actually works the way I hoped, not just in theory — it caught and recovered from a real stale link on its own during the same session I built it in.

VL53L0X: "no I2C devices found" traced to unsoldered header pins

  • Symptom: a fresh VL53L0X breakout scanned as completely empty on the I2C bus, across a long list of ruled-out causes: XSHUT floating vs. tied high, VIN at 3.3V vs. 5V, SDA/SCL swapped, alternate GPIOs, a slower I2C clock, even a second brand-new sensor.
  • Cause: the header pins were never soldered, just pushed through the board's through-holes and resting against the breadboard. A multimeter continuity check passed every time, because pressing a probe onto the pin forced a contact that wasn't present in the board's resting state.
  • Confirmed by: running the I2C scan in a tight loop while physically pressing on the header pins, the sensor showed up correctly at its real address, 0x29, during sustained contact, and dropped out the instant contact broke.
  • Fix: solder the header pins. No wiring or firmware change was needed: the pin assignments and test sketch were already correct.

The lesson I keep relearning: a continuity check with a multimeter only proves a connection is good while the probe is pressed on it. It can't catch a joint that's only bad under the board's own resting weight. The WebSocket bug above was the same shape of problem in a totally different layer of the stack: everything looks fine right up until you actually watch it fail.

The sim physics only broke once I plugged in real numbers

I built the MuJoCo model with placeholder dimensions first, checked it didn't explode, then swapped in the robot's real measurements, and it immediately did explode: the chassis flung across the room at multiple meters per second, spinning at 30+ radians a second within a single 5-millisecond physics step. Two bugs, both hidden by the placeholder numbers being small enough to get lucky. First, the formula placing the caster wheel had chassis-length terms that canceled out algebraically to nothing, leaving just a sign error that put the caster behind the driven wheels instead of in front, harmless with an 8cm placeholder offset, but with the robot's real 15cm offset it left the whole front of the chassis unsupported. Second, even after fixing that, the wheel motors were applying enough instantaneous torque to spin up against the wheels' tiny rotational inertia that the reaction alone was enough to throw the chassis. Fixed by capping the actuator torque to something a small motor could realistically produce, instead of letting the physics solver assume infinite torque was available to hit the target speed instantly.

Same lesson as the VL53L0X header pins, really: a check that passes under one set of conditions (small placeholder numbers, a probe pressed on a pin) doesn't mean the underlying thing is actually correct. It just means you haven't hit the case that exposes it yet.

Current status

Where things stand right now: the rebuilt 2-motor chassis drives cleanly in both directions and pivots in place over Bluetooth, both verified reliable across repeated live trials after fixing two separate motor-stall bugs. The camera and the VL53L0X are fused, and a point-blank reading now hands off to the firmware's own class-agnostic backup-and-turn recovery instead of just stopping — implemented this session, not yet re-tested live against a real obstacle. The SenseCAP touchscreen panel is flashed, renders correctly, and holds a live connection to the bridge with a real-time status feed. Bluetooth staleness, previously a manual-restart-and-hope problem, now has a real fix: a heartbeat watchdog that caught and recovered from a genuine stale link on its own during testing. On the sim side, the trained policy reliably avoids obstacles it's never seen before (2 collisions out of 400 randomized layouts, zero out of a fixed set of deliberately hard ones), after finding and fixing a sensor bug that had every earlier run training blind, and a reward-design flaw that let continued training quietly trade safety for speed. It hasn't touched the real robot yet, and there's still a real tradeoff between a faster/more confident checkpoint and a more cautious/more reliable one that I haven't fully closed.

Next up: get an IMU installed and start taking real motion measurements (turn rate, speed) to replace the sim's remaining placeholder values and properly fix a backward-drive veer I'm deferring rather than eyeballing again; re-test the point-blank recovery handoff live against a real obstacle; build the bridge that lets a trained sim policy actually drive the real robot; close the safety/performance gap in the reward design; and eventually look at whether the sim should train against a more realistic model of the real robot's actual sensing limits before starting on the swarm part — multiple robots coordinating, which is the whole reason this is called Chakra and not just "one robot." Right now it's one robot. The swarm is still ahead of me.
