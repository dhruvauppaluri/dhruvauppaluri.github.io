Apollo 1 — EEG Brain-Wave Controlled Rover

A hand-built four-wheel rover driven by neurological intent: Muse S focus levels over Bluetooth, a multi-agent edge AI stack on Weave and Redis, and motor commands that scale with how locked-in you are.

github.com/dhruvauppaluri/axiom

Project Overview

  • Event: WeaveHacks 4 (24-hour build, June 2026)
  • Role: hardware builder — assembled the 4WD chassis, motor drive electronics, and wiring; integrated servo, ultrasonic, and camera mounts on the team’s rover design
  • Key hardware: Arduino Uno, four-motor H-bridge drive with independent transistor stages, SG90 servo, HC-SR04 ultrasonic, camera for object detection, Muse S EEG headband
  • Stack: multi-agent LLM system traced through Weave (Weights & Biases), Redis streams for live signal ingestion, Python host daemon, Arduino firmware
  • Repository: github.com/dhruvauppaluri/axiom

Objective

The goal at WeaveHacks was to close the loop from thought to motion — not a joystick, not a keyboard, but sustained focus on a Muse S headband translated into real-time drive commands on a rover we built from scratch in a day. Apollo 1 is that rover: the team’s EEG-to-actuator pipeline intercepts focus levels over Bluetooth, normalizes them against a stored baseline, and maps the result to proportional motor PWM on four independent wheel channels.

My part was the hardware — not designing the full stack solo, but building the platform the stack needed to run on. Not a kit car: a 4WD chassis I wired with per-motor transistor switching so each wheel’s speed could track focus smoothly instead of snapping on or off.

System architecture

  • EEG acquisition: Muse S streams over Bluetooth; focus level (β/α band ratio) is the primary control axis.
  • Signal conditioning: multi-agent pipeline normalizes live EEG against a calibrated baseline, filtering ambient noise to isolate intentional focus shifts.
  • Edge orchestration: Weave traces the neural → agent → command path; Redis handles stream ingestion and pub/sub between the brain-state engine and actuators.
  • Motor layer: Arduino firmware receives focus-scaled commands and drives four wheels through independent transistor switching circuits — PWM duty proportional to measured focus.
  • Perception: servo-mounted ultrasonic ranging plus a camera-based object detection pipeline — the rover can locate targets and execute behaviors (circling, avoiding, or approaching) when focus is held long enough.
  • Fallback control: USB serial and BLE paths in the firmware repo for bench bring-up when the EEG link or power budget was fighting us.

Implementation

Robot hardware

I built the 4WD platform during the hackathon — chassis, motor mounts, drive electronics, and the physical integration for the servo, ultrasonic sensor, and camera. Each motor got its own transistor switching stage so speed could be modulated independently and proportionally to focus level rather than treated as a single on/off bus. Motor outputs are forced LOW at boot so the rover does not jerk on reset; that mattered when I was probing stages that were getting hot enough to smell wrong.

Brain signal to motor command

The team’s pipeline runs focus level from the headband through baseline normalization and into real-time motor commands on the Arduino I flashed. Higher sustained focus → higher duty cycle on the drive channels; drop below threshold → coast or stop. Separating “user is actually focusing” from “EEG is noisy because someone walked past” was the software side’s fight; my job was making sure the wheels actually responded when those commands arrived.

Perception and behaviors

A servo sweeps an ultrasonic sensor for ranging; a camera feed feeds an object detection pipeline on the host. With focus held, the rover can circle a detected target, back off, or close distance — behaviors gated on sustained neurological intent rather than a button press.

Host software

The open repo carries the Arduino sketches, a Python daemon for BLE/USB bridging, and bench tools like probe.ino for when a transistor leg is wrong and firmware looks guilty. Weave instrumentation on the agent side made it possible to see where latency or bad baselines were killing the demo.

Debugging & iteration

Power — the main bottleneck

The biggest systemic issue was not the EEG pipeline or the motor code — it was sustaining power. Four motors, a servo, radio modules, and the logic overhead of proportional drive pull hard on the supply. We started on 9 V batteries because that is what we had on the bench at 2 AM.

They were not enough. Under load, rail voltage sagged enough that PWM looked fine in software while wheels barely turned, and the Arduino would brown out when all four channels demanded current at once. I spent a long stretch with a DMM probing the supply at the battery terminals, at the regulator input, and at each motor node — watching voltage collapse under drive versus at idle.

That tracing made the failure mode obvious: high internal resistance and poor conversion efficiency on disposable 9 V cells, not a mystery bug in the focus mapper. The fix I am planning is a LiPo pack with proper current delivery and a regulator sized for simultaneous motor load — better conversion, lower sag, and enough headroom to run the perception stack without the rail dipping every time focus ramps speed.

Motor wiring and bring-up

  • Cause: a bad solder joint or wrong transistor leg looks like “firmware is broken” until you read the node and see 0 V when you expected 5 V.
  • Fix: live voltage commands via probe.ino, per-pin drive tests, and a SCAN mode that walks motor outputs while reporting analog readings.
  • Outcome: separate “is the circuit alive” from “is the protocol wrong” before chasing EEG or agent logs.

Reflection

Apollo 1 is the physical half of what we brought to WeaveHacks — a rover that actually moves when your brain says go, built in twenty-four hours on a table covered in jumper wires. I did not design the whole system alone; I built the hardware that made the demo real — chassis, drivers, wiring, and the bench tools that kept us from guessing when something did not move.

The part that stuck with me is power engineering showing up late. I came in focused on getting motors to track PWM; I left knowing that a marginal battery can waste an afternoon of integration work. LiPo is the next hardware revision, and I will size it from measured stall current instead of hope.
