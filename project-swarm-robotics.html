Autonomous Swarm Robotics (In Progress)

A self-driving rover that avoids obstacles, recovers dropped connections, and can be controlled from a touchscreen—the first step toward a multi-robot swarm.

View the repository

Overview

The long-term goal is a group of small robots that can coordinate. I am starting with one rover because a swarm is only useful if each robot can first drive, sense obstacles, and recover from common failures on its own.

  • My role: solo hardware, firmware, control software, interface, and simulation work.
  • Robot: two-wheel ESP32 rover with a camera and short-range distance sensor.
  • Controls: autonomous vision, a browser controller, and a separate touchscreen panel.
  • Status: one rover works; multi-robot coordination has not started.

Swarm, in plain English: several robots sharing information and dividing work. This project currently proves the first robot, not the swarm itself.

What I built

I rebuilt the chassis from my Apollo 1 rover around two driven wheels and one free caster. The simpler layout removed a recurring motor-channel fault and made turning more dependable.

A camera recognizes common obstacles, while the distance sensor stops or backs away from anything nearby—even objects the camera cannot name. A touchscreen and browser share the same control bridge, and manual commands temporarily override autonomous driving.

The touchscreen’s low-level display setup is adapted from the board’s community reference software. My work is the Wi-Fi connection, control interface, live status, and multi-robot tab structure.

What I learned

  • Motor starts: both motors demanded too much current at the same instant. Starting them a few milliseconds apart stopped one wheel from stalling.
  • Lost connections: Bluetooth sometimes appeared connected while no data moved. A regular heartbeat now detects silence and reconnects automatically.
  • Vision blind spots: the camera ignored objects outside its trained categories. The distance sensor now triggers a backup-and-turn response without needing to know what the object is.
  • Physical connections: a sensor passed a probe test only because the probe pressed an unsoldered pin into contact. Soldering the header fixed the real problem.

Learning in simulation

I also built a digital version of the rover in MuJoCo and trained a driving policy with reinforcement learning—software learns by receiving rewards for safe progress and penalties for collisions.

Early results looked good even when the rover only rocked in place, because the reward measured movement instead of useful forward progress. Another run drove blindly because the simulated sensors pointed backward. Watching the behavior and checking raw sensor values exposed both problems.

After correcting the sensors and rewarding safer behavior, the policy collided in 2 of 400 randomized test layouts and in none of the fixed difficult layouts. It has not driven the real rover yet.

Current status

The first rover drives forward, backward, and turns reliably. Camera and distance sensing work together, the touchscreen shows live status, and the Bluetooth watchdog has recovered a genuinely stalled connection.

Next I will measure real speed and turning with an added motion sensor, retest close-range avoidance, and connect the trained policy to the physical rover. Only after one robot is dependable will I begin coordination between multiple robots.
